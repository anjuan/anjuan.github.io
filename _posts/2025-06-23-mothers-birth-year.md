As a technology leader, I spend most of my time thinking about the future - artificial intelligence, quantum computing, the next big programming paradigm. However, sometimes looking backward provides the most clarity about where we are and where we need to go.

## A Time Machine to 1947

If I could travel back to 1947, the year my mother was born, I would encounter a radically different America. Not ancient history, not the distant past, but within living memory - my own mother's lifetime.

Let's boot up our temporal debugging console and examine what was legal in 1947:

```python
class America1947:
    def check_rights(self, person):
        if person.race == "Black":
            return {
                "library_access": False,
                "university_admission": False,
                "housing_choice": False,
                "business_service": False
            }
```

## The Infrastructure of Inequality

### Libraries
The same libraries where I discovered my love for technology and science could have legally barred me from entry based solely on my race.

### Higher Education
- The University of Texas at Austin, where I earned my electrical engineering degree? Legal right to deny admission.
- Texas A&M University, where I completed my MBA? Same story.

### Housing
The banks that later provided my home loans could legally use redlining to prevent Black families from buying homes in certain neighborhoods.

### Public Spaces
Supermarkets, theaters, restaurants - places I frequent without thought today - all had the legal right to refuse service based on race.

## From Illegality to Reality

The life I live today - my career in technology, my home ownership, my educational achievements - would have been illegal for me to obtain in my mother's birth year.

Let that sink in:
- My mother was born into a world where my current life was illegal
- These restrictions aren't ancient history
- They existed within living memory
- Many who enforced them are still alive today

## The John Lewis Test

When John Lewis spoke about what was "legitimate" versus what was "legal," he wasn't making a legal argument - he was making a moral one.

Consider:
- Laws can be unjust
- Legality doesn't equal morality
- Justice transcends legislation
- Progress requires recognizing this distinction

## The Persistence of Time

Why does this matter in 2025?

1. **Context Matters**
   - Current inequities have historical roots
   - Systems don't appear from nowhere
   - Yesterday's laws shape today's reality

2. **Progress is Recent**
   - Many barriers fell within living memory
   - Change is possible but requires work
   - Progress isn't guaranteed

3. **Legacy Effects Continue**
   - Wealth gap persistence
   - Educational disparities
   - Housing segregation
   - Healthcare inequities

## Conclusion

My mother's birth year isn't just a date - it's a measuring stick for progress and a reminder of how recently certain Americans were legally barred from basic rights.

When we understand that these restrictions existed within living memory, we:
- Gain perspective on current challenges
- Appreciate progress more deeply
- Recognize the work still needed
- Commit to continued improvement

The journey from 1947 to 2025 shows both how far we've come and how far we have yet to go.

Remember: Something can be legal but illegitimate, just as something can be illegal but just. The arc of history bends toward justice, but only when we actively bend it.
