Org charts are getting flatter. In May 2026, Forbes reported that the average span of control had grown from 8.1 direct reports per manager in 2013 to 12.1 in 2025, and it cited one projection of about 25 by 2028. The same piece cited a 2025 Korn Ferry survey in which 41% of employees said their organization had cut management layers, and 37% said the lack of managers left them feeling directionless. And in LeadDev's June 2026 report, 22% of engineering leaders said they'd seen fewer manager roles in their organizations.

I understand why companies do this. Layers slow decisions down, and some managers really do just forward email. However, I've noticed that when the managers go away, the management work doesn't get the memo.

Think about a family doctor. If you double her patient load and keep the workday the same length, nobody gets better care. Visits get shorter, waits get longer, and the things that should have been caught early get caught late. Doubling a manager's span without changing the job does the same thing to a team. The status reporting might survive. The coaching won't.

I've seen both ends of the span question. My first lead role, around 2000, was an interface team of roughly four to six people on a utility's CRM rollout. I wrote the workplans, ran a weekly team meeting, and knew what every person was working on. A few years later, I led an interface team of about 30 people on a large telecom program, drawn from several parts of my firm, plus contractors and an offshore group. Thirty direct relationships was far too many for one person to do well, so I built leads inside the team and leaned on them hard.

Even with leads, the status work was the easy part to share. I could hand out task assignments and progress tracking. Knowing how each person was actually doing was much harder to hand off, and it's the part that suffers first when a span gets too wide.

Years later, I worked at a remote-first company where engineering managers were called coaches, and each of us had a handful of engineers. That small span is a big part of why I could run every 1:1 with a roadmap of monthly intentions for each person. At 12 people, that gets hard. At 25, it's a fantasy.

So, what should leaders do if spans are widening anyway?

First, decide on paper what the manager's job is now. AI can absorb a lot of status reporting, and it should. It can't absorb a career conversation, a conflict between two senior engineers, or the quiet signals that someone is about to quit. If the job description still includes all of that at twice the span, the job is broken.

Second, find out who picked up the work. When managers go away, coaching tends to land on senior engineers and tech leads who never signed up for it. Some of them will be great at it. Few will be recognized for it. Put it in their reviews, or watch it quietly stop happening.

Third, build leads deliberately. Give them real authority, let them stand in for you, and write down the credit they earn.

If your span just doubled, take an hour and list everything you used to do for each person. Then decide, item by item, who does it now. Some of it might still be you. Some of it should be someone else. None of it should be nobody.
