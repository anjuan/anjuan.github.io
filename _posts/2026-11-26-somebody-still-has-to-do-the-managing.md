Org charts are getting flatter. Managers are responsible for double the direct reports they handled a year ago, and individual contributors are feeling the loss of management attention. Some organizations are getting rid of managers altogether. 

I understand why companies do this. Layers slow decisions down, and some managers don't do their jobs very well. However, I've noticed that when the managers go away, the management work stays.

Think about a family doctor. If you double her patient load and keep the workday the same length, nobody gets better care. Visits get shorter, waits get longer, and the things that should have been caught early get caught late. Doubling a manager's number of directs without changing the job does the same thing to a team. The status reporting might survive, but the coaching, conflict management, and sense of shared vision won't.

I've seen both ends of the span question. In one of my first lead roles in the early aughts, I managed an interface team of roughly four to six people on a utility's CRM rollout. I wrote the workplans, ran a weekly team meeting, and knew what every person was working on. A few years later, I led an interface team of about 30 people on a large telecom program, drawn from several parts of my firm, plus contractors and an offshore group. Thirty direct relationships was far too many for one person to do well, so I built leads inside the team and leaned on them hard.

Even with leads, the status work was the easy part to share. I could hand out task assignments and progress tracking. Knowing how each person was actually doing was much harder to hand off, and it's the part that suffers first when you have too many people to track.

Years later, I worked at a remote-first company where engineering managers were called coaches, and each of us had a handful of engineers. That small span is a big part of why I could run every 1:1 with a roadmap of monthly intentions for each person. At 12 people, that gets hard. At 25, it's a fantasy.

So, what should you do if your organization is asking managers to support more people?

First, document what the manager's job is now. AI can absorb a lot of status reporting, and it should. It can't absorb a career conversation, a conflict between two senior engineers, or the quiet signals that someone is about to quit. If the job description still includes all of that at twice the number of direct reports, the job is broken.

Second, find out who picked up the work. When managers go away, coaching tends to land on senior engineers and tech leads who never signed up for it. Some of them will be great at it. Few will be recognized for it. Put it in their reviews, or watch it quietly stop happening.

Third, build leads deliberately. Give them real authority, let them stand in for you, and document the value of their impact.

If your number of direct reports just doubled, take an hour and list everything you used to do for each person. Then decide, item by item, who does it now. Some of it might still be you. Some of it should be someone else. None of it should be nobody.
